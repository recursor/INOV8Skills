# The Brain Dump Playbook: Extracting Tacit Knowledge from Subject Matter Experts

A practical, field-tested guide for interviewers tasked with pulling business-process knowledge out of someone's head and turning it into usable documentation. The techniques below are adapted from contextual inquiry (Beyer & Holtzblatt), Applied Cognitive Task Analysis (Militello & Hutton), the Critical Decision Method (Klein/Crandall), the Critical Incident Technique (Flanagan), think-aloud protocol research, journalism interview craft, and practitioner experience in instructional design and process documentation.

---

## 1. Pre-Interview Preparation

**Scope ruthlessly before you book a minute on anyone's calendar.** A "brain dump" with no boundaries produces a meandering monologue that no one can convert into documentation.

- **Define the deliverable first.** Are you producing an SOP, a training course, a runbook, or a transition handover? The artifact dictates the depth and format of what you elicit.
- **Pick ONE process per session.** "How to process a customer refund in our CRM" is scopeable. "How you do your job" is not.
- **Identify the audience for the documentation.** A novice replacement needs different detail than a peer cross-training. Tell the SME who will read the output — it changes how they explain.
- **Map what already exists.** Before the interview, gather: existing SOPs, screenshots, org charts, system access lists, sample work products (reports, tickets, emails), and any prior documentation. Read it. You don't have to be the expert; you have to ask non-trivial questions.
- **Build a rough task spine.** Sketch the process at a high level (3–6 steps maximum) from existing artifacts. You'll validate and correct it in the session — this is the start of an ACTA "task diagram."
- **Send a pre-read to the SME.** Include: purpose of the session, scope statement, your draft process spine, time estimate, what you'll record, and 3–5 anchor questions. This dramatically reduces ramp-up time and prevents the "all-I-can-dump-on-you" dynamic.
- **Set expectations explicitly.** Tell them: "I'll interrupt you a lot. I'll ask 'why' and 'what could go wrong' a lot. None of it is a test — it's how I extract things you may have stopped noticing you do."
- **Prepare materials:** a recording tool (with consent), a shared whiteboard or Miro/Lucid board, screen-sharing access if the work is on a computer, sample documents to react to, and a notes template with sections for steps, decisions, exceptions, tools, and unanswered questions.

## 2. Interview Structure and Flow

**Multiple shorter sessions beat one marathon.** Cognitive fatigue degrades elicitation quality fast, and experts surface forgotten details between sessions.

- **Recommended cadence:** 60–90 minutes per session, 2–4 sessions for a moderately complex role, spaced 2–5 days apart. Contextual inquiry sessions are typically scoped at 1.5–2 hours for the same reason.
- **Session 1 — Orientation & task diagram.** Walk through the process at the highest level. Goal: agree on the spine and identify the cognitively demanding steps that deserve deep dives.
- **Sessions 2–N — Deep dives.** One or two sub-processes per session, including exceptions, decisions, and edge cases.
- **Final session — Playback & gap-fill.** Walk a draft of the documentation back to the SME and pressure-test it.

**Sequencing options (pick based on the work):**
- **Chronological / "day in the life"** — best for role transitions and operational roles with a clear daily/weekly rhythm.
- **By process area** — best when the role contains multiple distinct workflows (e.g., a finance analyst doing close, forecasting, and ad hoc analysis).
- **By frequency** — start with the most common 80% of the work, then move to rare-but-critical events (audits, escalations, year-end). Don't skip rare events; that's where the irreplaceable knowledge lives.
- **By artifact** — walk through real recent outputs (a closed ticket, a delivered report) and reverse-engineer the process. This is one of the highest-yield techniques because it anchors abstract reasoning to concrete decisions.

**Opening the session (first 5 minutes):**
1. Restate purpose, scope, and how the output will be used.
2. Confirm recording consent.
3. Ask a low-stakes warmup: *"Before we get into the process, can you tell me how this work landed on your plate and how long you've been doing it?"*
4. Set the partnership frame (Beyer & Holtzblatt's contextual-inquiry principle): *"Treat me like an apprentice. I'll watch and ask questions; you'll show me how the work actually gets done — not how the manual says it should."*

## 3. Question Techniques

### Eliciting tacit knowledge — the "I just know it" stuff

Tacit knowledge is stored in procedural memory and resists abstract questioning. Stop asking "How do you do X?" and start using these techniques:

**Show, don't tell (Think-Aloud + Contextual Inquiry).** Have the SME perform the actual task while narrating. Share their screen. The instruction is: *"Do the work the way you normally do it, and say out loud everything you're looking at, thinking, and deciding — even things that feel obvious."* When they go silent, prompt with *"What are you looking at right now?"* or *"What just made you click that?"*

**The Knowledge Audit (ACTA probes).** Use these scripted prompts; they reliably surface expert cognition:
- *Past & Future:* "Have you ever walked into a situation and immediately known how it got there and where it was heading? Tell me about it."
- *Big Picture:* "What are the major elements you have to keep track of for this task?"
- *Noticing:* "Tell me about a time something 'popped out' at you that others missed."
- *Job Smarts:* "Are there ways of working smart on this that you've developed over time?"
- *Improvising:* "When have you had to deviate from the standard approach? What told you to?"
- *Self-Monitoring:* "How do you know when your own work on this isn't going well?"
- *Anomalies:* "When have you spotted that something was 'off,' even before you could say why?"

**Critical Incident Technique (Flanagan).** Ask for *specific, recent, concrete events* — not generalizations:
- *"Tell me about the last time this process went really smoothly. Walk me through it from start to finish."*
- *"Now tell me about the last time it went sideways. What happened, what did you do, and why?"*
- Probe both successes and failures. Memory is far more reliable for episodes than for abstractions.

**Critical Decision Method (working backwards).** Identify a recent complex case, then walk it backward in time. Recency bias works in your favor — the memory is sharper, and you reveal the *actual* decision path rather than the sanitized procedural version.

**Simulation / scenario probes.** Present a realistic situation in the present tense: *"You just got an email from a customer saying X. Walk me through what you do in the next 30 minutes."* Then drip-feed complications: *"Now you discover Y is also true. What changes?"* This activates procedural memory the way abstract questions can't.

### Surfacing decision criteria and heuristics

Stop the SME at every fork in the process and ask:
- *"What would have to be true for you to do A instead of B here?"*
- *"What are you looking at on the screen / in the email / in the data that tells you which way to go?"*
- *"If a new hire did this exact step, what would they get wrong, and how would you know?"*
- *"You said 'it depends' — what does it depend on? Give me three examples where it went different ways."*

The goal is to convert *"it depends"* into an explicit decision rule or flowchart. Expert judgment lives in micro-decisions, and unless you stop and dissect each one, they won't appear in your notes.

### Probing for exceptions, edge cases, and "what could go wrong"

- *"What's the weirdest version of this you've ever seen?"*
- *"What breaks this process? When does the standard approach fail?"*
- *"What do you check for before you hit submit / send / approve? What are you guarding against?"*
- *"If you got hit by a bus tomorrow, what's the one thing your replacement would absolutely get wrong?"*
- *"What mistakes do new people consistently make here?"* (Experts find it easier to articulate what others get wrong than what they get right.)

### Workarounds and unwritten rules

- *"Where does the official process not match what actually happens?"*
- *"Is there a system or person you have to work around? How?"*
- *"Who do you call when X breaks, and why that person?"*
- *"What's documented somewhere that's wrong or out of date?"*
- *"What do you do on Tuesdays / month-end / quarter-close that you don't do other times?"*

### The 5 Whys

Use it sparingly and laterally — not as an interrogation, but to drill from a surface step to the underlying reason. *"Why do you copy that field into the spreadsheet?" → "Because finance needs it in that format." → "Why that format?" → "Because their old system can't read CSV." Stop when "why" yields no new information.* Be careful: 5 Whys can lead down a single track and miss multiple causes — branch when needed, and avoid an accusatory tone (*"What led to that?"* lands better than *"Why did you do that?"*).

## 4. Active Listening and Follow-Up

- **Use silence aggressively.** Journalists call this the three-second rule: after the SME finishes a thought, wait. They almost always add the most valuable detail in the second pass.
- **Paraphrase back.** *"So what I'm hearing is — you check the dashboard first, and only if X is above threshold do you escalate. Is that right?"* Beyer & Holtzblatt call this the *interpretation* principle: you build a model out loud and let them correct it. *"No, that's not quite right…"* is gold.
- **Watch for vague language and pounce.** Words like *check, ensure, validate, review, make sure, normally, usually, sometimes, it depends, basically* are flags. Each one hides a specific behavior. Ask: *"What exactly do you check for?" / "Usually meaning how often — and what's the exception?" / "Walk me through one specific 'check.'"*
- **Convert abstractions to examples.** Whenever you hear a generalization, ask: *"Can you give me a recent example?"*
- **Don't lead.** Avoid *"So you just hit approve, right?"* Replace with *"What do you do next?"*
- **Track open threads visibly.** Keep a running "parking lot" list of things to come back to. Tell the SME what's on it so they know nothing is being lost.
- **Know when to dig.** Dig when: the answer is shorter than expected, body language shifts, the SME laughs or sighs ("oh, that's a whole thing…"), or the answer contradicts something they said earlier.

## 5. Capturing the Knowledge

**Default to recording — audio at minimum, video/screen share when the work is on a computer.** Get explicit consent up front.

- **Record + take notes.** Recording is your safety net; live notes force you to listen for structure. Don't try to transcribe verbatim during the session — you'll stop listening.
- **Transcribe afterward.** Modern AI transcription (Otter, Fireflies, Zoom, MS Teams) is accurate enough that there is no longer a good reason to skip this step. Tag the transcript with labels like `[STEP]`, `[DECISION]`, `[EXCEPTION]`, `[WORKAROUND]`, `[TOOL]`, `[FOLLOW-UP]`.
- **Have them demonstrate, not describe.** Whenever the work touches a screen, screen-share and screen-record. A 10-minute Loom of someone actually doing the task captures sequence, timing, hand movements, and verbal context that text never will. AI tools (Scribe, Tango, ScreenApp, Guidde) can convert these recordings into draft step-by-step SOPs with screenshots.
- **Build visuals live.** Sketch the process flow on a shared whiteboard *as the SME talks*. Decision diamonds for forks, swim lanes for handoffs. Showing them the diagram triggers corrections and surfaces missing branches. Photograph or save the diagram.
- **React-to artifacts.** Ask the SME to open a recent real example (a closed deal, a finished report, a resolved ticket) and walk you through *why* each element of the artifact is the way it is. Concrete artifacts force articulation of implicit quality standards.
- **Use a structured note template** with consistent fields: Step, Trigger/Input, Action, Tool/System, Decision criteria, Output, Exceptions, Who else is involved, How you know it's done right.

## 6. Common Pitfalls

- **The curse of knowledge.** Experts have chunked thousands of micro-decisions into automatic procedures. They literally cannot see steps they take. Your job is to reconstruct what's invisible to them. Counter with: think-aloud, simulation, watching real work, and asking *"What would a new hire get wrong here?"*
- **Accepting "it's obvious" or "you just know."** This is the single most important moment in the interview. Respond: *"Let's pretend I'm the person replacing you in three months. What do I need to see, hear, or check to 'just know' it?"*
- **Glossed-over steps.** Watch for jumps in the narrative. *"Then I run the report"* — between *what* and *run*, there are usually 6 sub-steps. Slow them down.
- **The sanitized procedure.** Experts often describe the official version, not what they actually do. The fix is contextual observation: watch them work and ask about deviations in real time.
- **Leading questions and confirmation bias.** Avoid asking questions that imply an answer. Don't paraphrase in a way that smooths over inconsistencies — surface them.
- **Over-collecting, under-synthesizing.** A 4-hour transcript is not documentation. Schedule synthesis time equal to or greater than interview time.
- **Single-source risk.** Where possible, interview a second practitioner — variations between experts reveal both the true core of the process and where judgment legitimately differs.
- **Treating exit interviews as knowledge transfer.** Exit interviews capture feelings about the employer; knowledge transfer captures operational know-how. They're different conversations with different audiences.
- **Blank-page paralysis.** Asking a departing SME to "write down everything you know" produces either nothing or a surface-level dump. Always use guided extraction, never a blank document.
- **Interviewer fatigue and rushing.** When you're tired, you stop probing. End the session.

## 7. Post-Interview Synthesis

**The interview produces raw material; documentation requires deliberate synthesis.**

1. **Within 24 hours**, while context is fresh: skim the transcript and tag content. Extract every step, decision, exception, tool, and "follow-up needed" item into a structured outline.
2. **Build a Cognitive Demands Table** (from ACTA) — one row per cognitively difficult step, with columns for: difficulty/why it's hard, cues experts rely on, common errors, and strategies. This becomes the backbone of training material and the "judgment layer" that pure step-by-step SOPs miss.
3. **Draft the artifact in the form your audience needs:**
   - SOP / runbook → step-by-step with screenshots, decision points called out, exceptions in a sidebar.
   - Decision flowchart → for judgment-heavy work where "it depends" needs to become explicit branches.
   - Mistake-prevention checklist → derived from the "what new hires get wrong" probes.
   - Video walkthrough → for software-driven work; pair with a written index for searchability.
4. **Identify gaps explicitly.** Mark every unresolved point with `[GAP]` or `[CONFIRM]`. Don't paper over uncertainty with confident prose.
5. **Run a playback session.** Walk the draft back to the SME (live, not async — async review of long docs almost always returns silence or rubber-stamps). Read it aloud or have them read it to you. Watch for: "well, but…", "actually it's more like…", and silent skips. Each is a correction.
6. **Validate with the recipient.** If a successor or trainee is available, have *them* try to perform the task using only the documentation. Every place they get stuck is a documentation defect. This is the single most reliable test of whether the brain dump worked.
7. **Set a refresh cadence.** Documentation decays. Assign an owner and a 6–12 month review trigger. Capture process changes by re-recording the affected segment, not by rewriting from scratch.

## Quick-Reference Question Bank

Keep these in your back pocket; they work in almost any session:

- *"Walk me through the last time you did this — start to finish."*
- *"What are you looking at right now? What just made you decide that?"*
- *"What would have to be true for you to do something different here?"*
- *"What do new people consistently get wrong on this step?"*
- *"What's the weirdest version of this you've ever seen?"*
- *"Where does the official process not match what actually happens?"*
- *"If you got hit by a bus tomorrow, what would your replacement absolutely miss?"*
- *"You said 'usually' — what's the exception?"*
- *"How do you know you're done? How do you know it's right?"*
- *"Show me, don't tell me."*

The throughline of every technique above is the same shift in stance: stop asking experts to *describe* their work in the abstract, and instead get them *doing, remembering, or reacting* — then interrupt, slow down, and dig into the moments that look automatic. That's where the knowledge you came for actually lives.
