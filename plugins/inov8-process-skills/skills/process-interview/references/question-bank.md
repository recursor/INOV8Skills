# Question Bank

A working library of probes, organized by elicitation goal. Pick 1–3 per turn, never the whole list. The probes assume the SME owns the process and Claude is the interviewer; phrasing is for text interviews.

## Setting the frame

Use these in the first 5 minutes of any session.

- "Before we get into the process, how did this work land on your plate, and how long have you been doing it?"
- "If you handed this off to a brand-new hire on Monday, what's the part you'd worry most about them getting wrong?"
- "What's the version of this process the official documentation captures, and where does the real version diverge?"

## Building the spine (Phase 3)

- "From start to finish, what are the major phases of this process? Just 3–6 high-level steps — we'll go deep on each one later."
- "If a colleague asked you 'what do you do all day', and you had only one breath, how would you describe this process?"
- "What kicks this process off — what's the trigger or input that starts it?"
- "How do you know the whole process is finished? What's the final output?"

## Critical Incident Technique (CIT — Flanagan)

Anchor every probe in a specific, recent, concrete event. Memory is far more reliable for episodes than for abstractions.

- "Tell me about the last time this process went really smoothly. Walk me through it from start to finish."
- "Now tell me about the last time it went sideways. What happened, what did you do, why?"
- "What's the most recent example you can remember where you had to think hard about a step? Walk me through it."
- "Pick a case from the last week or two that's still fresh — let's use that as our anchor."

## Critical Decision Method (CDM — Klein/Crandall)

Identify a recent complex case, then walk it backward. Recency bias works in your favor — the memory is sharper, and the *actual* decision path emerges instead of the sanitized version.

- "Take the most complex case you handled in the last month. Walk me through it backward — start with the outcome, and tell me how you got there."
- "At the moment you made decision X, what were you weighing? What other options were on the table?"
- "What did you know at that moment that someone less experienced wouldn't have known?"
- "If you'd missed cue Y, what would have happened next?"

## ACTA Knowledge-Audit probes (Militello & Hutton)

The reliable workhorse. Pick 2–3 per deep-dive step.

**Past & Future**
- "Have you ever walked into a situation and immediately known how it got there and where it was heading? Tell me about it."
- "When you look at a new case, what tells you what kind of case this is going to be?"

**Big Picture**
- "What are the major elements you have to keep track of for this task?"
- "What's the overall mental model you carry while doing this?"

**Noticing**
- "Tell me about a time something 'popped out' at you that others missed."
- "What do you notice in this work that a peer six months in wouldn't notice yet?"

**Job Smarts**
- "Are there ways of working smart on this that you've developed over time?"
- "What shortcuts have you earned the right to take?"

**Improvising**
- "When have you had to deviate from the standard approach? What told you to?"
- "What's an example of a case where the textbook answer would have been wrong?"

**Self-Monitoring**
- "How do you know when your own work on this isn't going well?"
- "What tells you to slow down or double-check?"

**Anomalies**
- "When have you spotted that something was 'off,' even before you could say why?"
- "What's a 'huh, that's weird' moment you can recall, and how did it resolve?"

## Decision criteria and heuristics

Stop at every fork. The goal is to convert *"it depends"* into an explicit decision rule.

- "What would have to be true for you to do A instead of B here?"
- "What are you looking at — on the screen, in the email, in the data — that tells you which way to go?"
- "If a new hire did this exact step, what would they get wrong, and how would you know?"
- "You said 'it depends' — what does it depend on? Give me three examples where it went different ways."
- "Walk me through three cases: one obvious go, one obvious no-go, one borderline. What separates them?"
- "What's the threshold? At what point does X stop being acceptable and start being a problem?"

## Exceptions, edge cases, what-could-go-wrong

- "What's the weirdest version of this you've ever seen?"
- "What breaks this process? When does the standard approach fail?"
- "What do you check for before you hit submit / send / approve? What are you guarding against?"
- "If you got hit by a bus tomorrow, what's the one thing your replacement would absolutely get wrong?"
- "What mistakes do new people consistently make here?"
- "What's the catastrophic-failure version — the one that would land in someone else's lap as a problem?"

## Workarounds and unwritten rules

- "Where does the official process not match what actually happens?"
- "Is there a system or person you have to work around? How?"
- "Who do you call when X breaks, and why that person specifically?"
- "What's documented somewhere that's wrong or out of date?"
- "What do you do on Tuesdays / month-end / quarter-close that you don't do other times?"
- "What rule did you learn the hard way that's not written down anywhere?"

## Simulation / scenario probes

Activate procedural memory by presenting a realistic situation in present tense.

- "You just got an email from a customer saying X. Walk me through what you do in the next 30 minutes."
- "It's Friday at 4:45pm and the system is showing Y. What do you do?"
- "A new hire pings you with the message Z. What do you tell them?"
- (Drip-feed complications mid-answer) "Now you discover that A is also true. What changes?"

## Artifact reaction

Concrete artifacts force articulation of implicit quality standards.

- "Open a recent real example — a closed ticket, a finished report, a sent email — and walk me through *why* each part of it looks the way it does."
- "What would make this artifact wrong? Show me what 'bad' would look like next to this 'good.'"
- "If you saw a junior version of this, what would your first edit be?"
- "What's invisible in this artifact that you put a lot of thought into?"

## The Five Whys (lateral, not single-track)

Use sparingly to drill from a surface step to the underlying reason. Branch when "why" yields multiple causes. Avoid an accusatory tone — *"What led to that?"* lands better than *"Why did you do that?"*

- "Why does that field have to be in that format?"
- "What led to copying it into the spreadsheet rather than referencing it?"
- "Was that always the case, or did something change?"
- (Stop when "why" yields no new information.)

## Closing the session

- "Of everything we covered today, what felt under-explained when you said it out loud?"
- "What did this session make you realize you do that you hadn't articulated before?"
- "What's still nagging at you that we should pick up next time?"
- "If you could only document one thing from today for your replacement, what would it be?"

## Quick-pocket question bank

The ten that work in almost any session:

1. "Walk me through the last time you did this — start to finish."
2. "What are you looking at right now? What just made you decide that?"
3. "What would have to be true for you to do something different here?"
4. "What do new people consistently get wrong on this step?"
5. "What's the weirdest version of this you've ever seen?"
6. "Where does the official process not match what actually happens?"
7. "If you got hit by a bus tomorrow, what would your replacement absolutely miss?"
8. "You said 'usually' — what's the exception?"
9. "How do you know you're done? How do you know it's right?"
10. "Show me, don't tell me — paste an example."
