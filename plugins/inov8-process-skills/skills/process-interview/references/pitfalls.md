# Pitfalls and Recovery Moves

The most common ways an elicitation interview goes wrong, with concrete recovery moves.

## 1. The curse of knowledge

**What it looks like:** the SME describes work in chunks, skipping micro-steps that have become automatic. Sentences like *"then I just process it"* or *"obviously you'd check the dashboard."*

**Why it happens:** experts have chunked thousands of micro-decisions into automatic procedures. They literally cannot see steps they take.

**Recovery moves:**
- Anchor in a *recent specific case*, not the abstract.
- New-hire framing: *"What would a new hire get wrong here?"* (Easier for experts to articulate than what they get right.)
- Slow them down on jumps: *"Between 'open the report' and 'send it,' walk me through the actions in order."*
- Refuse "obvious": *"Pretend I'm replacing you in three months. What do I need to see, hear, or check to 'just know' it?"*

## 2. Accepting "it's obvious" or "you just know"

**What it looks like:** the SME hits a step that involves judgment and says *"you just know"* or *"it's obvious from the data."*

**Why it matters:** this is the single most important moment in the interview. Tacit judgment is the highest-value content and the most easily missed.

**Recovery moves:**
- *"Pretend I'm the person replacing you in three months. What do I need to see, hear, or check to 'just know' it?"*
- *"Walk me through three cases where 'just knowing' kicked in. What was the pattern across them?"*
- Switch to **CDM walkback** on a recent complex case.

## 3. Glossed-over steps

**What it looks like:** narrative jumps. *"Then I run the report."* Between *what* and *run*, there are usually 6 sub-steps.

**Recovery moves:**
- "Before you run it, what happens between *finished entering data* and *clicking run*?"
- "There's a gap between step 3 and step 4 — what fills that gap?"
- If the SME compresses, mirror the compression back: "You said 'pull the data and submit.' Walk me through the *pull* part."

## 4. The sanitized procedure

**What it looks like:** the SME describes the *official* version of the process — the one in the manual, not the one they actually do. Often surfaces in the first session, when the SME is still presenting.

**Why it happens:** the SME is performing for an audience. They want to seem competent and on-procedure.

**Recovery moves:**
- Anchor every probe in a *real recent case*, not the abstract.
- Ask explicitly: *"Where does the official process not match what actually happens?"*
- Ask for artifacts: *"Open the most recent real example and walk me through it."*
- Probe for unwritten rules: *"What did you learn the hard way that's not written down?"*

## 5. Leading questions and confirmation bias

**What it looks like:** *"So you just hit approve, right?"* or *"That's the only exception, isn't it?"*

**Why it matters:** the SME accommodates. They confirm the prior assumption, and the rest goes unsaid.

**Recovery moves:**
- Replace closed leading questions with open ones: *"What do you do next?"*, *"What other exceptions have come up?"*
- Don't paraphrase in a way that smooths over inconsistencies. If the SME contradicts an earlier statement, surface it: *"Earlier you said A always happens before B. Just now it sounded like sometimes B comes first. Which is it?"*

## 6. Over-collecting, under-synthesizing

**What it looks like:** a 4-hour transcript with no structure. The interviewer plans to "synthesize later." Synthesis never happens cleanly because the structure was never built.

**Recovery moves:**
- Update the working file *during* the interview, not after.
- Tag content as it comes in: `[STEP]`, `[DECISION]`, `[EXCEPTION]`, `[WORKAROUND]`, `[TOOL]`, `[GAP]`.
- Mirror the spine back to the SME periodically and let them correct it — synthesis as you go.
- Schedule synthesis time equal to or greater than interview time.

## 7. Single-source risk

**What it looks like:** all the documentation comes from one SME. The output reflects their personal style, blind spots, and idiosyncrasies — not the actual process.

**Recovery moves:**
- Where possible, schedule a second interview with another practitioner.
- Surface the single-source risk explicitly in the deliverable's gaps section: *"This documentation reflects [Name]'s approach. Variations among other practitioners have not been validated."*
- During playback, ask: *"Is there a colleague who does this differently? What would they push back on?"*

## 8. Treating exit interviews as knowledge transfer

**What it looks like:** an SME is leaving the company, and the org tries to capture their knowledge in a 60-minute exit interview. The SME ends up venting about their employer instead of explaining their work.

**Why it matters:** exit interviews capture *feelings*, not *operational know-how*. They're different conversations with different audiences.

**Recovery moves:**
- Schedule knowledge transfer separately from any HR exit conversation.
- If departure is imminent, prioritize ruthlessly: which 1–2 processes are highest-risk if undocumented? Capture those first.
- Use guided extraction (this skill), never a blank document.

## 9. Blank-page paralysis

**What it looks like:** the SME is asked to "write down everything you know about your job." They produce a surface-level dump or nothing at all.

**Why it happens:** without structure, the cognitive load is unbearable. The SME doesn't know where to start, what counts, or what to skip.

**Recovery moves:**
- Always use guided extraction, never a blank document.
- Provide the spine first (Phase 3), then probe each step (Phase 4).
- If the SME insists on writing first: ask them to draft only the *task spine* (3–6 steps). Then schedule an interview to probe each step.

## 10. Interviewer fatigue and rushing

**What it looks like:** late in a long session, the interviewer accepts shallow answers, stops probing, moves on too quickly.

**Why it matters:** the second half of a fatigued session produces the worst data and the most rework.

**Recovery moves:**
- End the session at 60–90 minutes. If the interview itself feels rushed, the SME feels it too.
- Audit interviewer warning signs: shorter follow-up questions, accepting "yeah, basically" without probing, skipping vague-language flags.
- Reschedule rather than push through.

## 11. Marathon sessions

**What it looks like:** a single 3–4 hour "brain dump" with one SME. Cognitive fatigue degrades elicitation quality fast — by the third hour, both parties are running on fumes.

**Recovery moves:**
- Split into 2–4 sessions of 60–90 minutes each, spaced 2–5 days apart.
- The space between sessions matters: experts surface forgotten details and corrections between sessions.
- If a single session is forced (departure, timezone), explicitly take 15-minute breaks every hour.

## 12. Over-reliance on the recording

**What it looks like:** the interviewer doesn't take live notes because "we're recording it." Live listening drops; the SME notices; energy fades.

**Recovery moves:**
- Recording is the *safety net*. Live notes force the interviewer to listen for structure.
- Don't transcribe verbatim during the session — that stops listening too. Capture structure: spine, decisions, exceptions, parking lot.
- Tag the working file as you go, not after.

## 13. Missing the "huh, that's weird" moments

**What it looks like:** the SME laughs, sighs, or says *"oh, that's a whole thing…"* and the interviewer moves on.

**Why it matters:** these are signals of expert cognition that the SME has not yet articulated. The "whole thing" almost always contains a high-value heuristic, exception, or workaround.

**Recovery moves:**
- Treat every laugh, sigh, or qualifier as a dig-cue.
- Stock follow-up: *"That sounded like there's a story there. What's the 'whole thing'?"*
- If the SME deflects, park it explicitly and come back later: *"I want to hear about that — let me park it for now and pick it up before we wrap."*

## 14. Failing to validate with the recipient

**What it looks like:** the SOP gets written, signed off by the SME, filed away. No one ever tries to *use* it. Six months later, a new hire fails on it and no one connects the dots.

**Recovery moves:**
- The single most reliable test of whether the brain dump worked: have a successor or trainee try to perform the task using *only* the documentation. Every place they get stuck is a documentation defect.
- Make this validation step part of the deliverable's acceptance criteria, not optional.
- Capture the trainee's stuck-points and update the documentation before "shipping" it.

## 15. No refresh cadence

**What it looks like:** the documentation is finalized, then forgotten. Two years later, half of it is wrong because the systems, tools, or process changed.

**Recovery moves:**
- Assign an owner and a 6–12 month review trigger.
- Capture process changes by re-recording the affected segment, not by rewriting from scratch.
- Note the *"Last reviewed"* and *"Next review due"* fields prominently in the document header.

## Quick recovery cheat sheet

| You hear / notice | Recovery |
|---|---|
| "it's obvious" / "you just know" | New-hire framing + CDM walkback |
| "usually" / "normally" / "sometimes" | "How often, and what's the exception?" |
| "it depends" | "Give me three examples that went different ways" |
| Narrative jump | "What happens between X and Y?" |
| Sanitized procedure | "Where does the official process not match what actually happens?" |
| SME laughs or sighs | "There's a story there — what's the whole thing?" |
| Short answer where depth was expected | Park, return with CIT anchor |
| SME contradicts earlier statement | Surface it directly, gently |
| Interviewer talking more than the SME | Stop. Ask one open question and wait. |
| Vague-language word | Pounce immediately (see active-listening.md table) |
